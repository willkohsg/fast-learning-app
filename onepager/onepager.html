Sec 3 A-Math — The Night Before

Syllabus 4049 · EOY Paper 1 · Formulas not on the formula sheet, and the traps that actually cost marks

Read this first

The formula sheet gives you the quadratic formula, the binomial expansion, the trig identities and the sine/cosine rules. Everything below is NOT on it. If you only have twenty minutes, learn the red boxes — they are where real marks were lost in marked scripts, not where the hard questions were.

Discriminant — the whole of it

D = b² − 4ac
D > 02 distinct real roots; line cuts curve twice
D = 0equal roots; line is a tangent
D < 0no real roots; no intersection

"Always positive" (curve above the x-axis for all x): a > 0 AND D < 0.
"Always negative": a < 0 AND D < 0.

Curve above a line? Subtract first: (curve − line) > 0 for all x, then apply the rule to that quadratic.

Trap — solving in k

k(k − 12) < 0 gives 0 < k < 12 (inside).
k(k − 12) > 0 gives k < 0 or k > 12 (outside).

Sketch the parabola every single time. Below the axis = between the roots. This one line of drawing has saved 4 marks more than once.

Surds

Conjugate of a + √b is a − √b — flip the sign of the surd only.

Denominator 2√3 − 3 → multiply by 2√3 + 3, giving 12 − 9 = 3.

Rational ≠ integer. If it asks for a + b√3 with a, b rational, a fraction like 11/6 is the expected answer — do not round it.

Surd equations: isolate one surd, square, isolate again if needed, square again. Then test every root in the ORIGINAL equation. Squaring invents roots. Sometimes both are valid — do not reject one out of habit.

Polynomials

Factor theorem: (x − a) is a factor ⇔ f(a) = 0.
For (2x − 1), use f(½), not f(2).

Remainder theorem: remainder on dividing by (x − a) is f(a).

Two conditions → two simultaneous equations in the two unknowns.

"Only one real root": factor out the linear part, then show the quadratic factor has D < 0. Say it in words to earn the mark.

Partial fractions

(ax + b)Aax+b
(ax + b)²Aax+b + B(ax+b)²
(x² + c) irreducibleAx+Bx²+c

Improper first. If the numerator's degree ≥ the denominator's, divide out a quotient BEFORE splitting. Degree 3 over degree 3 gives a constant quotient.

If the denominator is given unfactorised, factorise by grouping first: x³ − x² + 4x − 4 = x²(x−1) + 4(x−1).

Trap — logarithm laws

log A + log B = log(AB)  — NOT log(A+B)
log A − log B = log(A/B)  — NOT log(A−B)
n log A = log An

Change of base: logbx = logaxlogab  ·  logxa = 1logax

log9x = ½ log3x  ·  log4x = ½ log2x

Always check the domain. Every argument must be > 0, so reject roots that make any bracket zero or negative. The base must be > 0 and ≠ 1.

Indices & exponentials

Hidden quadratic: let u = ax.
Watch the stray factor: 32x+1 = 3(3x)², not (3x)².

ax is always positive — reject any negative value of u, and say why.

Growth/decay M = M0ekt: two readings → divide the equations to kill M0, then substitute back for M0. If neither reading is at t = 0, M0 is NOT the first mass given.

ln M0 is not M0. Undo the logarithm at the end.

Binomial theorem

General term: Tr+1 = nCr an−r br

In (2 + kx)6, the x³ coefficient is 6C3 2³ k³ — keep BOTH the power of 2 and the power of k. Dropping the 2n−r is the single commonest error.

Term independent of x = the x0 term: set the total power to zero and solve for r.

Bracket in front? (2 − x)(1 + 2x)8: the x⁵ term takes two contributions — 2×(x⁵ term) and (−x)×(x⁴ term).

Perpendicular bisector = midpoint + negative reciprocal gradient. Every point on it is equidistant from both ends — that one sentence proves a triangle isosceles.

"Solutions by accurate drawing will not be accepted" means show algebra. A measured answer scores zero even if it is right.

Linear law

Rearrange into Y = mX + c, then say plainly what is plotted against what.

y = axblg y against lg x — log both
y = abxlg y against x
y = x/(ax+b)1/y against 1/x, or x/y against x

Read gradient and intercept off the DRAWN LINE, using two points far apart. Never go back to the raw table.

If the intercept is lg a, you still need a = 10intercept.

Trap — trig signs

ASTC from quadrant 1: All · Sin · Tan · Cos are positive.

√(1 − p²) is NEVER 1 − p. Test it: p = 0.6 gives 0.8, not 0.4. A root does not distribute over a subtraction.

sin(90° ± A)= cos A
cos(90° + A)= −sin A
sin(180° − A)= sin A
cos(180° − A)= −cos A
tan(180° − A)= −tan A
Any (360° − A)quadrant 4: only cos stays +

sec, cosec, cot: find sin / cos / tan first, then take the reciprocal LAST.

Trig graphs & equations

For y = a ± b sin(nx): amplitude b, period 360°/n (or 2π/n), max a + b, min a − b.

A minus sign swaps where max and min happen — y = 3 − 4cos(x/2) starts at its MINIMUM.

Before sketching, ask: how many cycles fit the given range? Period 4π over 0 to 4π is exactly one.

Solving: transform the RANGE too. If 0 ≤ x ≤ 4π and the angle is x/2, then work in 0 ≤ x/2 ≤ 2π and double at the very end.

Negative cosine → quadrants 2 and 3, so 180°−α and 180°+α. Negative sine → quadrants 3 and 4. Getting this backwards costs both accuracy marks.

Proving an identity: work on ONE side only. Cross-multiplying both sides assumes the result and scores nothing.

Marks you can bank tomorrow

  • Answer the form asked for. "Exact" means surds, π or ln — never a decimal. "3 s.f.", "2 d.p.", "as a multiple of π", "integers", "rational" — a right number in the wrong form loses the accuracy mark.
  • Ceiling answers. "Least number of complete hours/days" — 8.94 becomes 9, not 8. Say the quantity first passes the target during that unit.
  • "Explain why" is worth ~2 marks a paper. Write a sentence, not just algebra.
  • "Hence" means use the previous part. Starting again wastes time and may score zero.
  • Never leave a part blank. Write the general term, the formula, the first substitution. Method marks are given for the attempt.
  • Keep unrounded values in the calculator for the next part; round only the final answer.
  • Re-read the last line against the question. Most marks in marked scripts were lost on the FINAL step of a correct solution — the wrong variable reported, the wrong form, a copied-down slip.

Tonight & tomorrow

Tonight: read the red boxes twice. Do not start a new topic — you will not learn it in one evening and it will cost you sleep, which is worth more marks. Pack your calculator (check the batteries), a ruler, a sharp pencil for graphs, and your ID.

In the exam: ~1.5 minutes per mark. A 12-mark question deserves 18 minutes; a 4-mark opener should take 6. If a part is stuck after two minutes, move on and come back — later parts are often independent.

Last five minutes: check every answer has the units, the form and the sign the question asked for. That sweep is worth more than one more attempt at a hard part.
